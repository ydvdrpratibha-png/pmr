How Agentic AI is Quietly Impacting the Foundations of Academic Research and Grant Funding

Two recent studies, read side by side, reveal something both exciting and deeply alarming about where AI agents are taking academic science and what researchers, funders, and institutions need to do about it.

PY
Pratibha Yadav, PhD
Science Communication Consultant

A researcher sits down to apply for a major grant. Instead of spending weeks agonising over every word, they feed their published work, the funder's criteria, and the texts of recently funded grants into an AI agent. Within minutes, they have ten polished, fully formatted proposals to choose from. The agent has already reviewed the literature, framed the methodology, and optimised the narrative for the specific funding panel.

Meanwhile, across the same university, a colleague's agent is doing the same thing. Multiply that by every researcher in every institution globally. Welcome to the grant-funding crisis that's already here.

Recently I came across two interesting literature pieces that pull back the curtain on what is happening at the intersection of agentic AI and academic science. What motivated me to write this blog was that while one article throws light on agentic AI and scholarly research, another one talks about agentic AI and research grants. When read together, they paint a picture that is exciting and at the same time deeply alarming.

The Core Tension

Power Without Guardrails

Agentic AI is not just a smarter chatbot. It is a fundamentally different class of tool. As Jaradeh and Auer define it in Source 1, an agentic AI system "iterates between natural-language reasoning with external actions taking API calls, executing code, and retrieving new documents, while iteratively revising its action plan." To put it in a nutshell, it plans, it acts, it learns from what it finds, and it keeps going until the goal is met. With minimal human input.

This capability is transforming the two most foundational activities in academic science: how research is discovered and synthesised, and how funding to do that research is applied for and awarded.

On the research side, the promise is genuinely extraordinary. But on the funding side, the numbers tell their own story. A 2026 analysis by Rees and Wilsdon (Source 2), drawing on data from 12 major funding bodies across Australia, Belgium, Canada, China, Spain, the UK, and the EU, found that every single one of those funders saw increases in grant application volumes between 2022 and 2025.

More proposals. Higher quality. Harder to differentiate. Grant reviewers are now being asked to make what Rees and Wilsdon describe as "largely arbitrary choices" about who to fund. This has a direct impact on the researcher in a more than ever shrinking funding ecosystem across the globe.

The Genuine Upside

What AI Agents Can Actually Do for Your Research

Let's talk about the genuine benefits, because they are substantial.

Jaradeh and Auer's research describes what they call "agentic deep search": autonomous agents that can formulate queries, harvest evidence from heterogeneous databases, evaluate source quality, and synthesise structured outputs. Systems like Elicit can rank and summarise hundreds of abstracts for evidence synthesis. Open-source tools automate significant portions of PRISMA-style systematic reviews. Patent prior-art searches that once took specialist teams can now be conducted in hours. Bibliometric trend analysis pipelines can classify thousands of papers to surface emerging research directions before they become mainstream.

For the working researcher, this matters. The hours you currently spend on the early-stage grind of literature searching are genuinely compressible. That time could be invested into the original thinking, the creative hypothesis, and the experimental design, the things that AI agents, despite their power, still cannot do.

But moving to the critical aspect, Jaradeh and Auer catalogue some serious failure modes that every researcher deploying these tools needs to understand before they trust the output.

Critical Risks

Things That Can Go Wrong

1
Paywall blindness

Agents can only access what they can access. Because they have no legal right to bypass subscriptions, they systematically over-sample open-access content. Bibliometric analyses confirm a 44% open-access bias in AI-retrieved citations. If the key paper in your field sits behind a JSTOR or Elsevier paywall, your agent probably doesn't know it exists.

2
Context limits

A systematic review's appendix can exceed 100,000 tokens. Model performance degrades as context stretches, with research showing sigmoid-like decay curves beyond 32,000 tokens. Your agent may be confidently summarising a document it cannot fully hold in mind.

3
Privacy exposure

If your research involves sensitive human data such as genomic sequences, patient notes, proprietary datasets, routing it through third-party AI endpoints can breach GDPR or institutional data governance frameworks.

"Use AI agents to compress the grind. Use your brain for the insight."

The Funding Side

What is Actually Changing for Grant Applicants

And be aware that funders are adapting. The UK Medical Research Council reinstated face-to-face interviews for all shortlisted applicants in early 2026, explicitly because of AI-augmented proposals.

The NIH has capped individual submissions at six per year.

Track record verification, portfolio assessments, and interview-based evaluation are coming back into fashion precisely because they are AI-resistant signals of genuine scientific thinking.

Your long-term career case rests on your ideas, your track record, and your collaborations. No agent can fake that. Build the real thing, use AI to amplify it, and make sure every output you put your name on is one you can defend, verify, and own.

What Needs to Change

Rebuilding Systems for an AI-Native World

Both research articles converge on a similar insight: the systems we have for funding, for peer review, for research synthesis were built for a pre-AI world. They need to be rebuilt, not patched.

For research agents to be academically trustworthy, Jaradeh and Auer identify eight non-negotiable requirements. The most actionable of these are:

Requirements for trustworthy research agents
  • Verifiability and traceability: every claim must link to a stable, auditable source, such as a DOI, a PubMed ID, a patent number.
  • Ethical and bias mitigation: agents must actively counter the tendency to over-represent English-language, high-impact, majority-demographic research. Bias-aware prompting has been shown to recover approximately 12% more non-English clinical trials compared with standard keyword filters.
  • Seamless workflow integration: tools must plug into existing academic infrastructure - Zotero, arXiv, Crossref.
  • Researcher-controlled narrative scope: agents need defined boundaries, so a cancer biology review doesn't drift into plant genomics.
  • Peer-reviewed source constraints: adding impact-factor filters and peer-review flags reduces noise by 35% without hurting recall.

For funding systems, Rees and Wilsdon advocate for a shift from proposal-quality evaluation to person-quality evaluation: track record verification, interviews, portfolio-based assessments.

The Bigger Picture

The Fork in the Road

"We stand at a fork in the road. One path turns LLMs' autonomy into an academic enhancer, reducing months of literature search into hours while still elevating the rigor of the scientific method. The other path, if unchecked, would broadcast misinformation at machine speed."

— Jaradeh & Auer (2025)

The same fork applies to funding systems. Either we build AI-native academic infrastructure with transparency, auditability, and human oversight at its core or we allow the race-to-the-bottom dynamics of optimised, homogenised proposals to hollow out what peer review and scientific funding is actually supposed to do: identify and support the best ideas.

Take-away Messages
For researchers, grant applicants, funders, and science communicators
01
AI agents are a tool, not a shortcut to your scientific identity

Your ideas, track record, and collaborations are what funders and peers ultimately evaluate. No agent can build that for you. Use AI to amplify what you have already earned not to substitute for it.

02
The grant system is already responding, be ready

Interviews, portfolio assessments, and application caps are returning because funders need AI-resistant signals of genuine scientific thinking. If you are not prepared to defend your proposal in conversation, the proposal itself may not be enough.

03
Know what your agent cannot see

A 44% open-access bias in AI-retrieved citations is not a minor footnote rather it is a systematic gap in your evidence base. Treat AI-generated literature reviews as a starting point, not a finished product.

04
Governance and infrastructure must catch up with capability

The tools exist. The research is clear. What is needed now is the institutional will to act on verifiability standards, on bias mitigation, and on funding evaluation frameworks before the system becomes unworkable.

05
Science communicators have a role to play here

As AI reshapes how research is discovered, synthesised, and funded, the ability to communicate science clearly and credibly becomes more valuable, not less. The signal that cuts through the noise is authentic human expertise and that still needs to be communicated well.
